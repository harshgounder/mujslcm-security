# MUJ SLCM Register Number Evidence and Enumeration Risk

## Executive Summary

- **Verified Login Grammar**: The official MUJ SLCM FAQ publishes the username template `name.registration number` and gives the example `garv.2506040062` [5] -> treat this as a confirmed syntax example, not as proof that every MUJ identifier follows the same visible digit partition.
- **MUJ-Generated Identifier**: The official first-year onboarding guide lists Registration No. as a pre-filled, non-editable Student Details field and labels it as generated by MUJ [6] -> model the number as an authoritative identifier, not as a student-selected value.
- **Separate Academic Fields**: The same guide separately lists Academic Year, Program/Branch, and Year of Admission [6] -> do not claim that `25`, `06`, or `04` encodes a year, stream, branch, or campus without a MUJ codebook.
- **Only One Direct Public Example**: In the reviewed public evidence, the direct printed ten-digit example is the FAQ sample `2506040062` [5] -> the evidence supports one public example, not a cross-year batch series.
- **Unverified Digit Semantics**: `25|06|04|0062` is a visually plausible analyst partition, but no reviewed MUJ source assigns meanings to those chunks -> report this only as an inference and keep batch, branch, stream, serial, and campus mappings unknown.
- **Identity Separation**: MUJ says first-year credentials are sent to the personal Gmail address, describes an official address pattern `name.XXXXXX@muj.manipal.edu`, and says the same password is used for SLcM and the official email [6] -> do not equate the six placeholder X characters with the registration number or assume that the public FAQ reveals a password.
- **Enumeration Math**: A ten-digit numeric string has a raw combinatorial space of 10^10 if leading zero strings are allowed, while fixing an assumed `25` prefix leaves 10^8 possibilities and fixing an assumed `250604` prefix leaves 10^4 possibilities -> these are candidate-space bounds, not counts of valid or active MUJ accounts.
- **Security Finding**: The public material establishes identifier predictability exposure, but it does not establish account existence responses, timing differences, rate limits, password-reset behavior, or authentication bypass -> the defensible assessment is a moderate information-disclosure concern with low confidence about exploitability until MUJ performs authorized internal verification.
- **Safe Research Boundary**: No login attempts, password-reset submissions, directory queries, timing measurements, header fingerprinting, or candidate testing were performed -> university researchers should use the evidence for defensive design review and coordinated disclosure, never for guessing or accessing accounts.

## 1. Scope, Evidence Standard, and Public-Only Boundary

The assessment concerns the public information surface around the MUJ Student Life Cycle Management portal at `mujslcm.jaipur.manipal.edu`. The official MUJ onboarding guide describes SLcM as the one-stop platform for academic information and points first-year students to the MUJ portal [6]. The public landing page itself exposes a MUJ portal welcome message, sign-in language, the `@muj.manipal.edu` identity context, and a parent-login label [3]. These observations establish a publicly visible authentication surface, not the behavior of the protected application.

The architecture values supplied in the research brief - ASP.NET MVC 5.3, .NET Framework 4.x, IIS 10, Akamai, and Microsoft 365 identity - are treated here as scope context rather than independently verified findings. This distinction matters. A public page can document a username convention without proving the framework version, reverse-proxy behavior, identity-provider flow, or authentication endpoint semantics. No active request was made to test those properties.

The evidence hierarchy is intentionally conservative. The official MUJ FAQ is the strongest source for the username grammar because it explicitly prints both the template and an example. The official first-year guide is the strongest corroborating source for how MUJ treats the registration number during onboarding. Official program pages are useful context for academic-year labels, but they are not registration-number codebooks. Public LinkedIn material can show how students describe education or activities, but a profile title is not an authoritative identity record. Generic Manipal or MAHE documents are not silently substituted for MUJ documents because similarly named institutions can use different application and student-number systems.

| Evidence surface | Direct observation | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Official MUJ SLCM FAQ | Publishes `name.registration number` and `garv.2506040062` [5] | Exact public username syntax and one illustrative sample | A universal parser, an active account, or digit meanings |
| Official MUJ onboarding guide | Lists Registration No. as generated by MUJ and lists academic fields separately [6] | Server-assigned identifier and separate profile attributes | That any number chunk encodes those attributes |
| MUJ SLCM landing page | Shows public portal sign-in context and parent login [3] | Existence of a public authentication landing page | Login success behavior, account enumeration, or rate limits |
| MUJ CSE program page | Shows curriculum labels for 2019-23, 2023-27, and 2025 onwards [4] | Public academic-year terminology | A relationship between those years and registration-number digits |
| Public profile material | The reviewed profile excerpt contains ordinary MUJ activity and profile metadata [14] | That public profiles can expose academic or activity context | An official registration-number codebook or a valid SLCM username |

The table's decision point is simple: the FAQ and onboarding guide are evidence of format and workflow, while the other surfaces are context or negative controls. The correct analytical posture is therefore high confidence about the published example and low confidence about any inferred internal encoding.

## 2. The Verified MUJ Username Grammar

The strongest result is direct and unusually specific. MUJ's official FAQ labels the registration link, then states `Username: name.registration number` and supplies `garv.2506040062` as the example [5]. The source therefore supports the following abstract grammar:

```text
username = name + "." + registration_number
```

The source does not state whether `name` means a first name, a preferred name, a normalized legal name, an Outlook alias, or another MUJ-managed value. The lowercase `garv` in the example demonstrates only how the example is printed. It does not establish case folding, punctuation handling, spaces, duplicate-name suffixes, or whether the name is selected by MUJ or by the student. Those are material unknowns for any count of accepted usernames.

The source also does not say that the registration number is always ten digits. The example contains ten numeric characters, which is an observation about the sample, not a published length rule. A responsible report must distinguish `the only public example observed has ten digits` from `MUJ requires exactly ten digits for every student`. The first statement is supported by the FAQ sample; the second is not.

The FAQ describes the password as being according to the official Outlook ID [5]. The onboarding guide adds that first-year credentials are sent to the personal Gmail address supplied during admission [6], that students must activate an official address shown in the placeholder form `name.XXXXXX@muj.manipal.edu`, and that the same password is used to log in to SLcM [6]. This creates a documented relationship among the onboarding email, official university email, and SLcM password. It does not reveal a password and does not prove that the registration number is the six-character portion represented by `XXXXXX`.

The registration number's authority is clearer than its internal grammar. The guide says the Student Details section contains non-editable fields pre-filled from admission information or generated by MUJ, including `Registration No.`, and explicitly annotates that field as `(Generated by MUJ)` [6]. A reasonable security design interpretation is that the identifier should be treated as an authoritative server-side attribute. That is an inference about the desired handling, not a claim about the implementation.

The `garv.2506040062` line should also be treated as an illustrative format example. The `eg:` marker makes it unsafe to infer that a real person named Garv owns an active account or that the displayed number can be used to authenticate. The public value is useful for format research, but it is not evidence of account status. This distinction prevents a public documentation example from being turned into a target list.

## 3. Anatomy of `2506040062`: Observable Characters Versus Unverified Meaning

The string supplied by the official FAQ can be described mechanically as a ten-character digit string. Analysts often split it as `25|06|04|0062` because the result looks like a two-digit year, two-digit code, two-digit code, and four-digit sequence. That split is an inference, not a MUJ-documented schema. There are many other possible partitions, including `250|604|0062`, `25|0604|0062`, or an opaque ten-digit value with no human-readable components.

| Observed portion | Possible analyst interpretation | Evidence status | Safe conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| `25` | Admission year, batch year, or cohort prefix | Inference only | It is the first two characters of the sample; no year mapping is published |
| `06` | Faculty, stream, school, or program code | Inference only | It is the third and fourth characters; no public codebook maps it |
| `04` | Branch, course, campus, or intake code | Inference only | It is the fifth and sixth characters; no public codebook maps it |
| `0062` | Student sequence, merit rank, or local serial | Inference only | It is the final four characters; no source calls it a sequence |
| `2506040062` | Opaque MUJ-generated registration value | Compatible with the evidence | MUJ generates the Registration No. [6], but opacity is an analyst model, not an explicit MUJ statement |

The most important counterevidence to a digit-decoding theory is the onboarding field layout. MUJ separately lists Academic Year, Program/Branch, and Year of Admission in Student Details [6]. That does not logically prove that the same values are absent from the registration number, because systems sometimes duplicate attributes in identifiers. It does mean that the public guide provides no basis for saying that a digit chunk is the authoritative copy of a field. The most that can be said is: the identifier and the academic attributes are separately represented in the documented workflow.

The public CSE program page provides another useful control against overinterpretation. It labels curricula as `2019-23`, `2023-27`, and `2025 onwards` [4]. Those are ordinary academic-program labels, not evidence that MUJ uses `19`, `23`, `25`, or `27` in a registration-number position. The difference between a visible curriculum label and a generated registration number is exactly why program-page dates cannot be used as a codebook.

The campus question is similarly unresolved. The official onboarding field list visible in the guide names Registration No., Student Name, Academic Year, Program/Branch, Date of Joining, and Year of Admission among other fields [6]. It does not state a campus-code field or explain any campus digits. That is not proof that MUJ has no campus identifier internally. It only means that a campus mapping is not documented in the material reviewed.

Accordingly, the report uses three labels. **Observed** means printed directly in the source, such as the exact example. **Supported workflow fact** means stated by MUJ, such as the number being generated by MUJ. **Inference** means a pattern suggested by the characters but not assigned by a source. Every claim that `25` is a batch, `06` is a stream, `04` is a branch or campus, or `0062` is a serial must remain in the third category.

## 4. Across Years, Batches, Branches, and Campuses

The research question asks whether the format persists across years and batches. The available public evidence does not establish that persistence. It gives one exact example and a general template, but it does not provide a second MUJ registration number from another admission year, a second program, or another campus. That is an evidence limitation, not evidence that the format changes.

| Research question | Public evidence located | Assessment | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is `2506040062` a documented MUJ example? | Yes. It appears in the official FAQ as part of `garv.2506040062` [5] | Confirmed as a published example | High |
| Is `25` a batch or admission-year prefix? | The guide separately lists Year of Admission and Academic Year [6], but does not connect them to the number | Not documented; any connection is inference | Low |
| Does the pattern repeat in 2024, 2025, or other cohorts? | No second direct example is established in the reviewed official corpus | Cross-year persistence is unknown | Low |
| Does `06` map to a stream or school? | The guide names Program/Branch as a field but publishes no numeric mapping [6] | No defensible mapping | Low |
| Does `04` map to branch or campus? | No reviewed source assigns that meaning; the guide does not describe a campus code in the visible field list [6] | No defensible mapping | Low |
| Is `0062` a serial number? | The FAQ prints it only as part of the example [5] | Serial interpretation is inference only | Low |
| Are admission letters a public codebook? | The guide lists the Admission Offer Letter as a mandatory upload [6] | It establishes an onboarding document, not public access to a codebook | Medium for document existence, low for format evidence |
| Do public LinkedIn titles document the number format? | The reviewed profile excerpt shows public MUJ activity/profile context [14] | It is not used as authoritative registration evidence | Low |

The admission-letter distinction is important. MUJ's guide requires students to upload an admission offer letter [6], so such letters are part of the documented onboarding process. That does not mean MUJ publishes every letter, that a publicly shared letter is authentic, or that a letter's application number is the same as the SLCM registration number. Application numbers, offer-letter identifiers, student registration numbers, and email aliases should be treated as separate namespaces unless MUJ explicitly links them.

The LinkedIn distinction is equally important. A public profile can expose a person's university, field of study, cohort dates, club roles, or profile slug, but those attributes are not a registrar-issued registration record. The reviewed excerpt is therefore useful only as a demonstration of public academic context, not as corroboration of `2506040062` or of any other numeric scheme [14]. Researchers should not mine profiles for personal identifiers simply because a username grammar has been published.

The current conclusion is a negative one stated narrowly: no reviewed public source supplies a verified table mapping MUJ registration-number digits to batch, stream, branch, course, or campus. That conclusion should not be expanded into `no such table exists anywhere`. The authoritative next source would be an MUJ registrar or admissions document that explicitly labels each position, or an internally approved data dictionary shared for the university security club's research.

## 5. Enumeration Math: Candidate Space Is Not Valid Account Count

The word `valid` needs careful definition. There are at least four different quantities:

1. A **published example** is a string printed in a public document.
2. A **syntactically plausible candidate** matches an analyst-selected pattern, such as ten digits.
3. A **valid registration number** belongs to MUJ's internal assigned set.
4. An **active username** is accepted by the live authentication system for a particular name and number.

The public FAQ proves one item in the first category: `garv.2506040062` [5]. It does not reveal the size of the second, third, or fourth categories. The following calculations are therefore bounds and scenario models, not a list of usernames and not instructions to test them.

| Scenario | Assumption | Candidate-space calculation | Result | Status |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Exact published example | Only the value printed in the FAQ is counted | 1 | 1 documented username string | Confirmed as a public example, not as an active account |
| Any ten-digit digit string | All ten positions can be `0` through `9`, including leading-zero strings | 10^10 | 10,000,000,000 | Raw syntactic upper bound only |
| Conventional ten-digit number | First position cannot be zero | 9 x 10^9 | 9,000,000,000 | Mathematical alternative; not a MUJ rule |
| Fixed assumed `25` prefix | `25` is fixed and the remaining eight positions vary | 10^8 | 100,000,000 | Inference-based scenario, not a verified batch count |
| Fixed assumed `2506` prefix | `2506` is fixed and the remaining six positions vary | 10^6 | 1,000,000 | Inference-based scenario, not a verified stream count |
| Fixed assumed `250604` prefix | `250604` is fixed and the final four positions vary | 10^4 | 10,000 | Inference-based scenario, not a verified branch count |
| Fixed entire sample | Every digit is fixed to the public example | 1 | 1 | One known string, not a population estimate |

The general formula is useful without pretending to know the codebook. Let `R` be the unknown set of MUJ registration numbers that the system actually assigns. Let `A` be the unknown set of active accounts. Then `A` is not derivable from the public example, and even `|R|` is not derivable unless MUJ publishes the allocation rules or a reliable population table. For an assumed prefix of length `k`, the raw digit-string space is:

```text
C_syntax(k) = 10^(10-k)
```

The valid set can only be a subset:

```text
|R_valid(k)| <= C_syntax(k)
```

The inequality may be very loose. MUJ could reserve ranges, skip numbers, assign numbers by a nonnumeric key, use multiple formats, or maintain separate identifiers for different populations. None of those possibilities is confirmed; they are reasons not to equate a combinatorial space with an account count.

For names, let `N` be the number of accepted canonical name strings in a chosen population and let `M` be the candidate number space. A simple syntactic Cartesian-product estimate is `N x M`. That product is not the number of valid accounts because the name and number may need to match, names may be normalized, duplicate names may be disambiguated, and many number candidates may never have been assigned. If the system accepts aliases or case variants, the username-string count may be larger; if it canonicalizes input, it may be smaller. The public documents do not resolve those rules.

The most defensible answer to `how many valid usernames are derivable` is therefore:

- **Directly documented**: one example string, `garv.2506040062` [5].
- **Syntactically modeled for an unpartitioned ten-digit field**: up to 10,000,000,000 digit strings, or 9,000,000,000 under the conventional no-leading-zero assumption.
- **Under the unverified `25|06|04|0062` hypothesis**: 100,000,000, 1,000,000, or 10,000 candidates depending on which prefix is treated as fixed.
- **Actually valid or active**: unknown.

The final category must stay unknown. Estimating it would require an authorized registrar data set or a controlled internal test plan, not public guessing. The math should be used to prioritize defensive controls and data minimization, not to generate candidate login requests.

## 6. Threat Model and Defensive Control Priorities

The public evidence creates a plausible identifier-discovery risk: a name plus a MUJ-generated registration number is visibly described as a username format [5]. If a student's name and registration number are independently exposed, an observer may be able to form a syntactically plausible username. This is an inference about predictability, not proof that the live portal confirms account existence or accepts the identifier.

OWASP's authentication guidance says an application should use a generic response regardless of whether the user ID or password is incorrect, the account does not exist, or the account is locked or disabled [11]. OWASP also warns that response processing time can differ by case and enable a timing-based attack [11]. Its testing guidance specifically recommends consistent generic responses and notes that response time, including the extra delay caused by an external forgotten-password service, can disclose whether a requested user is valid [9]. These are defensive requirements, not a claim that MUJ currently fails them.

The same logic applies to registration and recovery workflows. OWASP ASVS says valid users must not be deducible from failed authentication challenges, response messages, status codes, or response times, and that registration and forgot-password functionality require the same protection [8]. The Forgot Password Cheat Sheet recommends consistent responses for existing and non-existing accounts, timing consistency, and protection against excessive automated submissions [7]. NIST guidance likewise requires effective rate limiting where the authentication secret has less than 64 bits of entropy [10].

| Priority | Control objective | Why it matters for the MUJ pattern | Evidence to review safely |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Uniform authentication responses | Prevents a predictable username from becoming an account-existence oracle [11] | Approved code review, test-environment results, and response templates |
| P0 | Uniform response timing | Prevents differences caused by directory lookup, password verification, or email delivery [11][9] | Instrumented internal tests with synthetic accounts; no public probing |
| P0 | Generic forgot-password behavior | Keeps recovery from confirming whether a name/number pair exists [7] | Review recovery design, logs, and user-notification flow |
| P0 | Rate limiting and anti-automation | Limits repeated guesses and protects reset-token workflows [10][8][7] | Configuration review and authorized load tests in a controlled environment |
| P1 | MFA for sensitive access | OWASP identifies MFA as a strong defense against password-related attacks [11]; ASVS requires MFA at its L2 assurance level [8] | Identity-policy review and test accounts owned by MUJ |
| P1 | Recovery must not bypass MFA | ASVS requires a secure reset process that does not bypass enabled MFA [8] | Threat-model and workflow review |
| P1 | Authentication alerts | ASVS recommends notifying users about suspicious successful or unsuccessful attempts [8] | Notification policy and audit-log review |
| P1 | Credential-boundary review | MUJ's guide says the same password is used for official email and SLcM [6] | Confirm whether this is current policy, an initial-password instruction, or a federation behavior |

The identity coupling deserves special care. The official guide documents a same-password relationship, but it does not disclose whether the SLCM application validates directly against Microsoft 365, uses a synchronized directory, or merely instructs users to reuse a credential. Those are materially different architectures. The correct recommendation is to have MUJ confirm the authentication flow and ensure that password recovery, MFA, lockout, and alerting are enforced consistently across the linked services.

A predictable username is not itself a password compromise. It becomes more serious if the portal has distinguishable failure responses, weak default credentials, missing rate limits, recovery bypasses, or inadequate MFA. None of those conditions was tested here. The report therefore assesses the public format as an information-disclosure and enumeration-enabler concern, not as a confirmed unauthorized-access vulnerability.

## 7. Responsible OSINT Handling and Research Actions

The safest next step is not to search for more student identifiers. It is to obtain a sanctioned data dictionary or confirmation from the MUJ Registrar, Admissions, DMS, or IT security team. The official FAQ itself provides MUJ support contacts for login and SLCM issues [5], and the onboarding guide directs students to the Admissions Department for incorrect generated details [6]. Those channels are more authoritative than inferring semantics from public profiles.

A university security club can preserve the public evidence in a redacted evidence ledger with four columns: source URL, exact quoted observation, confidence, and unresolved question. The ledger should retain the official FAQ example because it is the core public observation, but should not add scraped names, profile identifiers, or guessed number variants. The club should also distinguish an illustrative example from an active account and should avoid publishing a larger list of candidate usernames.

| Safe research activity | Permitted purpose | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Review official MUJ PDFs and public pages | Confirm published wording and workflow | Do not submit credentials or forms |
| Ask MUJ for a codebook or test data | Resolve digit and campus semantics | Use synthetic or institution-provided records only |
| Review application code or logs with authorization | Determine response uniformity and identity flow | Do not inspect live student records without approval |
| Test in a MUJ-owned staging environment | Verify generic errors, timing, rate limits, and MFA | Written authorization, synthetic accounts, no production traffic |
| Report the public FAQ exposure to MUJ | Let the owner decide whether the example should remain | Do not frame the sample as proof of account compromise |

The onboarding guide's document-upload instructions also show why privacy handling matters: the guide lists admission offer letters, identity documents, marksheets, photographs, and other records among required uploads [6]. Those documents can contain far more sensitive information than the registration-number example. A public-information project should minimize collection, avoid reposting personal documents, and use only the smallest excerpt needed to establish the format.

The decision-ready action is to ask MUJ three questions: Is the registration number opaque or structured? Does it remain stable across program changes, campus changes, and readmission? Does the authentication and recovery layer return indistinguishable results for valid and invalid usernames? Public evidence can motivate those questions, but only MUJ can answer the implementation-specific parts responsibly.

## 8. Synthesis: What the Sources Reveal Together

The sources converge on a usability-security tension. The FAQ chooses a human-readable username formula that combines a name with a registration number [5]. That is convenient for onboarding because a student can understand the construction. The same choice can make identifiers easier to predict if the number allocation is sequential or if name and cohort information are public. The mechanism is therefore usability through derivation; the trade-off is that derivation can expose an account-discovery surface. This remains a risk model, not a confirmed live weakness.

The onboarding guide introduces a second, less obvious tension. It says MUJ generates the registration number [6], but it also displays academic year, program/branch, and year of admission as separate attributes [6]. The scope of the number is therefore unclear: it may be a stable person-level identifier, a cohort-level identifier, a program enrollment identifier, or an opaque database value. A stable person-level identifier is operationally convenient but can persist across systems; a program enrollment identifier can support academic administration but may change on transfer. The public source does not decide among those models.

The identity instructions add a third tension. MUJ sends credentials to a personal Gmail account, requires activation of an official `@muj.manipal.edu` address, and says the same password is used for SLcM and the official address [6]. This suggests an operational link between services, but it does not prove a shared backend. The benefit is simpler student onboarding. The trade-off, if the instruction represents actual credential reuse rather than federation, is a larger blast radius for password compromise. That sentence is explicitly an inference and requires MUJ confirmation.

| Dimension | Official FAQ | Official onboarding guide | Program/profile pages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Publishes a username grammar and one example [5] | Describes generated fields, credential delivery, and profile completion [6] | Exposes academic context such as curriculum labels or public activity metadata [4][14] |
| Scope | Login-name construction | Student lifecycle and identity onboarding | Public academic or social context |
| Evidence strength for number semantics | High for the exact example; low for digit meanings | High for MUJ-generated status; low for internal allocation rules | Low for registration-number semantics |
| Main trade-off | Easy-to-understand login versus predictable identifier grammar | Efficient onboarding versus possible credential coupling | Public transparency versus privacy and conflation risk |
| Time horizon | Immediate login usability | Lifecycle stability and account recovery | Long-lived public exposure after graduation or role changes |
| Safe action | Keep responses generic and avoid exposing account status | Confirm identifier lifecycle and authentication boundaries | Minimize publication and do not treat profiles as registrar records |

The most important divergence is between what is public and what is operationally authoritative. The FAQ is authoritative enough to prove the visible username syntax, but not enough to prove how the database generates numbers. The program page is authoritative for the fact that MUJ publishes curriculum-year labels, but not for registration-number encoding. LinkedIn is useful for understanding public context, but it is not a registrar system. Treating all three as equivalent would create false confidence.

The sources also support a practical design principle: if MUJ wants human-readable onboarding, it should not rely on secrecy of the username. The authentication layer should assume that names and registration numbers can be known and should resist account discovery through generic responses, timing consistency, rate limits, MFA, secure recovery, and notifications [11][8][7]. Separately, MUJ should decide whether the registration number is a public identifier, a confidential identifier, or merely an internal key. That policy decision is more important than whether an analyst can draw a visually attractive split between `25`, `06`, `04`, and `0062`.

## VERDICT

**Overall confidence: Medium for the public-format finding; Low for digit semantics, cross-year generalization, and active-account enumeration.**

1. **Confirmed**: MUJ publicly documents the username pattern `name.registration number` and prints `garv.2506040062` as an example [5].
2. **Confirmed**: MUJ's onboarding guide describes Registration No. as generated by MUJ and lists it with separate academic and program fields [6].
3. **Not confirmed**: `25` is a batch or admission year, `06` is a stream or school, `04` is a branch or campus, and `0062` is a serial. The public evidence assigns none of those meanings.
4. **Not confirmed**: the pattern repeats across years, batches, programs, or campuses. One example cannot establish a sequence.
5. **Bounded math only**: one documented example exists; raw ten-digit spaces range from 9,000,000,000 to 10,000,000,000 depending on leading-zero assumptions; hypothetical fixed prefixes yield 100,000,000, 1,000,000, or 10,000 candidates. None is a count of valid or active users.
6. **Security assessment**: the public format creates a plausible username-discovery concern, but no live authentication or recovery behavior was assessed. There is no evidence in this report of unauthorized access, account compromise, or a confirmed vulnerability.
7. **Recommended disposition**: treat the registration number as potentially discoverable, keep authentication and recovery responses indistinguishable, enforce rate limits and MFA, confirm the identifier lifecycle with MUJ, and keep further research inside an authorized staging or data-review process.

The bottom-line answer is therefore precise: `garv.2506040062` is publicly documented as a MUJ SLCM username example, but the internal meaning of `2506040062` is not publicly documented by the sources reviewed. Any claim of a batch prefix, stream code, branch code, campus code, or exact number of valid usernames would exceed the evidence.
